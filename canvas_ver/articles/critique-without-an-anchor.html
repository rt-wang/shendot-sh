Critique Without an Anchor

December 9, 2024 · 2,369 words

Foucault's construction of the modern power is no doubt a complex one. His genealogical deconstruction of modernity has yielded numerous insightful philosophical and political ideas, offering a fresh perspective on the transition of power dynamics from pre-modern to modern societies. However, many critics have found his work to be troubled by ambiguity in commitments to presuppositions and use of concepts. Fraser highlights a critical flaw in Foucault's approach, arguing that his attempt to be politically engaged while maintaining normative neutrality is fundamentally incompatible with the very vocabulary he employs. This vocabulary, rooted in the modern episteme that Foucault critiques, undermines the coherence of his position. Fraser contends that Foucault's seemingly "one-sided, wholesale rejection of modernity" is unsatisfactory and thus is in need desperately of a normative framework that can distinguish between acceptable and unacceptable use of power. To speak in Foucault's defense, I propose to offer a more nuanced analysis than treating Foucault's attitude on modern power as simply a total rejection of modernity itself. To me, the revelation of power dynamics within modernity, coupled with the melancholic tone conveyed through Foucault's performative discourse on modernity within its own framework, is not a shortcoming. Rather, it constitutes the very critique that his rejectionist perspective is intended to provide. In this essay, I will begin with a brief overview of Foucault's conception of modern power. Next, I will examine Fraser's critique, focusing on the challenges she identifies in Foucault's rejection of modernity. Finally, I will present a two-part defense of Foucault, engaging with Fraser's interpretations to varying extents.

Foucault never discretely defines power. However, the distinction between pre-modern and modern power has been made concrete through his analysis in discipline and punish. What first emerged in punitive and regulatory institutions during the early modern period gave rise to a new form of power: disciplinary power. Unlike juridical power in the pre-modern period, disciplinary power uses techniques such as the gaze to construct and subjugate individuals as targets of power, normalizing them into docile and useful subjects. This mechanism subsequently extends and becomes the foundational basis for the global exercise of modern power, supplanting the predominantly repressive forms characteristic of pre-modern traditional power. However, Fraser contends that no power regime is exclusively negative; instead, power is fundamentally productive. In modernity, individuals are constructed by the normalizing and objectifying gaze of power. The positive feature of power, especially in its ability to construct individuals, is also how Foucault weaves the web of an ineliminability. Every positive form of life is inherently embedded within power structures, making it impossible to base critiques solely on their relationship to power.

Specifically regarding modern forms of power, Foucault contends that certain critiques of modern power remain ensnared within the very framework of modernity they seek to challenge, thereby obscuring its underlying characteristics. This stance is particularly evident in his seemingly anti-humanistic reproach, as illustrated by his remark: "We have to promote new forms of subjectivity through the refusal of this kind of individuality which has been imposed on us for several centuries" (Foucault, p. 785)". He intends to undercut what these previous critiques have reached in their attempts by destabilizing even the constitutive norms of modernity. In doing so, he aligns himself with the rejectionist tradition of thinkers like Heidegger and Nietzsche, radically " ‘totaliz(ing)’ critique so that it turns against itself(Fraser, p. 166)." He is thus able to interrogate the most fundamental layers of modernity, causing the overall structure of the Foucauldian conception of modern power to shift away from the pragmatic realm of politics and normative judgment. Instead, his analysis can be read as operating on a purely philosophical and metaphysical level, rendering it normatively neutral—a perspective with which Fraser concurs. Conversely, when addressing more localized instances, Foucault critiques modern power with a politically charged tone, particularly evident in his analyses of biopower and the treatment of the mad.

Fraser begins her analysis precisely at this point, highlighting the fundamental challenge in Foucault's work: the stark contradiction between his politically charged critiques and the ostensibly normatively neutral framework he employs. There are two readings in which Fraser presents difficulties to in her essay. Fraser contends that in essence, Foucault is trying to "bracket" normative frameworks. In other words, Foucault "will refrain from problematizing the normative validity of power/knowledge regimes. (Fraser, p. 275)" However, Foucault suspends the normative framework to varying degrees. Fraser distinguishes between a complete bracketing of all political norms and a more limited suspension that targets only liberal norms.

Fraser's critique of the first reading, full-scale bracketing, is as follows. She contends that Foucault does adopt this reading in various occasions where he classifies his own approach to power as "strategically and militarily, not normatively (Fraser, p. 282)." Foucault's characterization of power as strategic enables him to employ terminology that is typically regarded as normatively charged in a neutral manner. Fraser raises the simple counterexample of biopower where Foucault in fact uses the language in a non-neutral way and shows preferences to one side of the power asymmetry normatively. The depiction of biopower as a part of the class struggle and subjugation suffices for Fraser to express her dissatisfaction with the coherence in Foucault's perspective. This counterexample, however, is enabled by a higher level critique by Fraser. She identifies that the theoretical foundation to prefer either the subjugated or the subjugating is unavailable to Foucault. It seems to her that under the bracketing of all political norms, it is impossible to identify on which side of the power structure is preferred. Without a normative framework, Foucault is unable to address the fundamental question: "What is wrong with modern power?" Consequently, Fraser contends that without answering this question, the theory lacks impact, as its entirely neutral description fails to convey its significance or guide meaningful critique.

Fraser also rejects the interpretation that Foucault merely dismisses liberal norms instead of challenging all available political norms. Fraser argues that because Foucault deploys liberal terms such as domination and subjugation in his work, to suspend a liberal framework successfully, he needs to situate himself in a new "post-liberal" framework that adopts these vocabulary. Otherwise, the words are simply unavailable to him. What fundamental is at odds here is Fraser's fundamental belief that without a normative framework, there is no critique and that Foucault "fails to appreciate the degree to which the normative is embedded in and infused throughout the whole of language at every level (Fraser, p. 284)." This is also emphasized when Fraser is engaging with Foucault's anti-humanism. Especially when treating Foucault's rejectionist position normatively, She contends that Foucault is not equipped with the vocabulary to oppose the "fully panopticized, autonomous society (Fraser, p. 182)" because he does not provide an alternative normative framework.

Fraser contends that Foucault's excessively broad conception of power creates significant challenges. By using power as an umbrella term that is undifferentiated, Foucault makes it impossible to develop nuanced normative positions, resulting in a one-dimensional and ostensibly neutral framework. Therefore, Foucault is compelled into a position devoid of the critical power and vocabulary necessary to effectively condemn the fallacies of modernity. Fraser is deeply unsatisfied with this conclusion and demands a normative framework from Foucault so that his rejection of modernity can be justified.

Having outlined Foucault's rejectionist position on modern power and Fraser's subsequent critique, I will now delve deeper into the specific dissatisfactions articulated by Fraser. To further substantiate Fraser's claim, she argues that Foucault's rejection of modernity is unjustified. Fraser argues that Foucault's current framework lacks the critical power necessary to underpin his rejectionist stance on modernity. And the only viable solution for Foucauldian theory to gain any sorts of critical power lies in adopting a normative framework that is coherent with Foucault's existing body of work. Operating under Fraser's first reading of Foucault where she rejects all political norms in modernity, my critique of Fraser's reading is two-fold.

Firstly, one could argue in Foucault's defense that Fraser's dissatisfaction does not significantly challenge his theoretical framework. One can read Foucault in a way that the critical power desired in Fraser's critique is in fact unnecessary. To quote Gillian Rose, Foucault's theory of modern power is "a theory which abolishes itself as theory, an ultimate self-perficient nihilism (Rose, p. 172)." Although This interpretation is meant as a critique for Foucault, it illuminates a particular structure in which Foucault's critique of modernity, following the rejectionist tradition that Fraser concurred earlier on in her work, is functioning. That is, by revealing the power structure in modernity, Foucault intends to destabilize the readers and make them aware of the unsettling "constructed as unconstructed" elements in our modern life. Rose and Fraser characterize aspects of Foucault's work as pure nihilism, particularly in areas where his theoretical authority diminishes, and it is precisely within these domains that his critique resides. In a sense, Fraser and Rose can be seen as interpreters of Foucault, as they experience the profound "stuck-ness" within the inescapable power relations that modernity imposes. Their response—a call for solutions—does not diminish the impact of Foucault's theory; rather, it effectively illustrates the contemporary predicament we face.

However, I do want to recognize that this form of critique perhaps is not what Fraser is looking for. In her "Young Conservative" essay, Fraser—although she does not categorize Foucault as a young conservative—argues that he has not provided sufficient evidence to demonstrate that his rejectionist position is superior to the dialectical approach. She contends that this inadequacy stems from his lack of the reformative, "leftist" values that she deems essential. While the dialectical approach can make normative claims by incorporating humanist language, Foucault's framework lacks this normative power. It is true that with my interpretation of Foucault's framework of modernity, her critique largely remains true. Foucault's critique does not offer a reformative motivation the same way the dialectical approach does. Dialectical criticism can pinpoint which power is legitimate and which power is not while Foucauldian critique can't. In this regard, Fraser acknowledges Habermas' critique of Foucault and grounds her own dissatisfaction in her normative framework, which evaluates the value of critical theories of power primarily based on their capacity to motivate "change". Overall, my defense of Foucault asserts that he embraces the form of critical power he delineates through his analysis of modern power. This critical power fosters a sense of "stuck-ness" and highlights the unsettling all-encompassing nature of disciplinary power.

Secondly, I will argue that even if her dissatisfaction stands, Foucault still possesses critical power of rationality that does not require a normative framework. My defense stems form a different reading of Foucault regarding his attitude towards truth. Fraser argues that Foucault's rejectionist position rejects the most fundamental norms of modernity which even includes "the very commitments to truth, rationality, and freedom that alone make critique possible (Fraser, p. 166)." Fraser treats Foucault's rejection to truth in an absolute sense where "Foucault aims to show that 'truth is not naturally free' (Fraser, p. 178). However, I contend that this represents another instance in which Foucault employs a term with excessive generality. At the end of Fraser's essay on Foucault's analysis of modern power. As I have capitulated earlier, she recognizes that Foucault has the tendency to use power in an all-encompassing manner which has caused the loss of normative nuances. To concur with her opinion, Foucault's rejection of truth can be read as not a rejection to truth as a whole, but just truth as confessional truth. In the same sentence where Fraser reads Foucault's understanding of truth as not naturally free, she proceeds to say that this particular truth is in contributions to the "centuries of coercion and intimidation to 'breed a confessing animal.'(Fraser, p. 178)" In my understanding, Foucault maintains that fundamental rational truths (e.g., "P is not not P") remain valid independently of social constructs. At the same time, he acknowledges that the discourse constructs bodies of knowledge that shape social truths. Thus, his critical power can reside in this one ahistorical thing that is available to pinpoint contradictions. In his anti-humanist framework, Foucault's primary critique is that defining humans as inherently rational beings is undermined by the mad. He argues that portraying humans as unconstructed entities is deceptive and, regardless of whether this portrayal is normatively judged as good or bad, the assertion that "humans are rational by nature" is fundamentally flawed. Thus, another defense of Foucault's critical power within his theory of modernity involves emphasizing the philosophical and logical dimensions of his critique rather than the normative judgments that necessitate an alternative framework different from liberal values.

In conclusion, the tension identified by Fraser in Foucault’s framework arises from the distinctive manner in which he undertakes the critique of modern power. Fraser’s dissatisfaction highlights what she sees as a central contradiction: Foucault’s language appears politically charged and yet he refuses any normative framework, leaving him seemingly unable to distinguish acceptable from unacceptable exercises of power. She argues that without this normative grounding, Foucault cannot justify his rejection of modernity or clarify what exactly is wrong with it.

Yet, as I have argued, this very absence of a normative anchor need not be read as a failing. Foucault’s approach reveals the unsettling mechanics of modern power—its capacity to normalize, subjugate, and produce docile subjects. His performative discourse illuminates the depth and pervasiveness of power relations, creating a sense of “stuck-ness” in which critique is part and parcel of the very structures it interrogates. This “nihilistic” dimension, frustrating though it may be, compels us to confront the historically contingent conditions under which truth might emerge.

Furthermore, even if Fraser’s dissatisfaction stands, I have suggested a second line of defense: that Foucault maintains a critical form of leverage grounded not in overt moral principles, but rather in a more nuanced understanding of truth and logical consistency. Interpreting his so-called "rejection of truth" as a rejection of historically contingent, confessional notions of truth—not truth as such—allows us to appreciate how his critique can operate on a philosophical level, independent of any specific normative framework.Thus, Rather than dismissing his approach as incoherent, we can instead recognize its capacity to unsettle our conventional understandings, prompting us to imagine new forms of critical engagement that extend beyond the confines of traditional normative discourse.
