Life a life final paper
Before I begin to articulate my argument on the meaning of life, I want to first clarify that this is a theory of coherence rather than truth. I believe that any understanding delivered by language is bound by rationality — here I mean linguistic structure — and thus is unable to capture foundationless a priori truth. To make this argument is out of scope, so I’m just going to simply posit it here. Although coherence might not be as ultimate as truth, it allows for an important construct in investigating the way we live: agency.

It’s phenomenologically evident that many of our thoughts come from outside our superficial consciousness. This exteriority is not just the random noise that passes through our mind when we are idle. It is also many of the “aha” moments that inform important decisions in our lives. The biggest realizations often come at unexpected times and places. It is only through rationality and the making of connections between these exteriorities that our consciousness is evoked. And the process by which these connections are made is rational. One might object by saying that if rationality exists as a Platonic ideal and there are correct and incorrect inferences between these exterior events, isn’t agency still not achieved here? Aren’t we still bound by a right answer? My response would be that here the reasoning and connecting of the dots are not deductive, but rather abductive. The process of reasoning is not to reconstruct the most real picture of how and what happened, but rather to construct a new self that is understandable by the consciousness of the now. I contend that it is only through rationalization and clear consciousness that we act as subjects; otherwise, we are just passengers in the backseat.

With these presuppositions in mind, I claim that, without access to some sort of alternative consciousness — which I believe is possible — to understand the meaning of life is to construct a coherent theory that anchors action. When meaning is understood as a particular objective or goal of life in a teleological framework, it fails to capture the exteriority that I mentioned above. It is impossible for our conscious framework to anticipate what comes into it. A fixation based on what we currently perceive seems unable to capture the variances of the future. In a sense, consciousness is both reactionary and constructive. We catch what’s thrown at us and make sculptures out of it. Thus, it is prudent to consider the meaning of life as something different. I contend that it is only possible to understand the meaning of life as the construction of a coherent system through which one understands oneself. The process of gaining clarity on one’s being through construction is one of rationality and thus the only one of agency. Therefore, as a subject, to experience oneself as a subject is to act with rational intent.

A difference with Murdoch is worth flagging here. Her attention is realist: the gaze is "just" because it is directed at an individual reality that is there to be seen correctly. My account is coherentist; the constructive activity does not aim at any external truth about the self but at intelligibility within the conscious system itself. Yet the structural point that subjectivity emerges through continuous rational activity rather than through punctual choice holds on either reading. Murdoch's realism gives her attention a target; my coherentism gives it a function. On either view, agency is the continuous activity, not the moment of choice.

One might consider my coherentist take to be asking too much — no one is coherent at all times. I'm simply not asking that. Coherence exists as a series of loss and discovery, through which we continue to evolve as life proceeds. One grasps a sentence only when one reaches the end of it, but once grasped, the holding of the meaning exists outside of time. Similarly, one could grasp the coherence of one's life synchronically while holding it outside of time. It is only when new experiences challenge the current system that one is required to change. What is held, in each grasping, is the fruit of the continuous rational activity of a self coming to perceive itself.

Aristotle’s theory of happiness is particularly relevant here. I agree with his trichotomic division of perception: a subjective perceiver, such as awareness or memory; a connection between perceiver and object; and an exterior object or activity. For him, the highest form of good is achieved when one perceives the rational activities of oneself. A rational act of oneself allows for the best connection through intelligibility. This rhymes with my personal experience very well. It is empirically true for me that a decision made with serious rational consideration is mostly satisfactory regardless of the consequence. To understand an act and fully commit to it is to accept all the possible outcomes it can lead to. This perhaps is an unusual take on rational decision-making. However, it seems to me that a rational finite being should be at ease with any undesirable outcome, since the future is never guaranteed and this should already be a factor in his consideration. Rationality does not necessarily lead to the best possible outcomes. The best possible outcomes happen to those who possess “blessedness,” as coined by Aristotle. Such a quality is outside conscious agency and thus cannot be studied as an object for a system of life, unless one is in some mythic alternative consciousness, which could exist. Thus, it seems true that without intelligibility and reason, one has provided space for guilt, regret, and fear in the muddiness.

One more important issue I think I should address is that of death. I contend that this picture of living life reduces the fear of death to just its biological urge. Just as I have argued above, rational decision-making is one without expectation of the consequences. Death, as the ultimate consequence of human life, should thus also not be a factor of fear when the intelligibility of a decision is achieved. A coherent system should have already factored in the possibility that one may die at any moment. In a way, the production of discourse around meaning is a feature of the living and bounded by death. Parallel to how Heidegger thinks about finitude without the infinite, we should also not concern ourselves with what’s on the other side and should focus on what’s here.

Thus, what grounds the meaning of life is not a measure at the end, nor is it the legacy that one leaves behind. Rather, it is the perspective that one possesses in order to truly realize one's subjectivity through the continuous actions of understanding oneself. It is neither purely diachronic nor purely synchronic; the coherence must hold whole at every moment, even as new and old ideas continuously rebuild it. Just as we are ever-evolving beings, my theory of coherence will develop too, and one day it might no longer look very much like this essay. But I thoroughly believe that the process of loss and discovery through intelligible actions is what will ground meaning. And I am sure that, if I am lost, I will find myself again.
