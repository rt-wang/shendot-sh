Wittgenstein final essay
In the Blue Book, Wittgenstein rejects the classical picture on which meaning consists of a dead sign animated by a living sense. He contends instead that the use of a word constitutes its meaning. This is not a reduction to behaviorism. It is a diagnostic move: the demand for an inner ingredient beyond the sign, Wittgenstein argues, is a philosophical illusion generated by the grammar of "meaning" itself. In this essay, I defend Wittgenstein's account against what I take to be its strongest challenge: the worry that two speakers could communicate flawlessly while associating radically different inner contents with their words, suggesting that use cannot exhaust meaning. I argue that this challenge gains its force only by presupposing the very picture Wittgenstein has dismantled. The supposed residue of meaning that use fails to capture is not a residue of meaning at all. Once a sensation or mental image enters language, what enters is not the inner item but a public practice that neither requires nor excludes any particular inner accompaniment.

Wittgenstein's use account arises from a concrete observation. In the Investigations, he examines the word "game." Many disparate activities count as games in ordinary language, and speakers deploy the term fluently and without confusion. Yet it is unclear that "game" admits of a unified definition. Board games, card games, ball games, Olympic games, children's games — what feature do they all share? Some involve competition; some do not. Some involve skill; some are pure chance. Some have winners; some do not. Wittgenstein's suggestion is that instances of games stand in relations of family resemblance: just as members of a family share overlapping physical features without any single feature common to all, games share overlapping characteristics without any defining essence (PI, 66–67). Some games share nothing direct with others and yet still belong to the family. This pattern, Wittgenstein argues, extends to most ordinary concepts. The platonist picture on which a word's meaning is fixed by a definition shared by all its instances fails as a general account.

What Wittgenstein proposes is an inversion of the direction of explanation. Rather than seeking an immaterial sense that unites every use of a word, we look at the uses themselves. The desire to unify them under a single definition is not merely unfulfilled but misconceived: family resemblance has no shared core to find, and any proposed definition breaks the moment a novel application is admitted into the concept. The competence we actually exhibit when using a word does not rest on grasping such a core. It rests on participation in the practice. Someone who has learned how "game" is used (who can apply it to new cases, recognize borderline ones, and follow others' application) has grasped its meaning, and there is nothing further to grasp behind this.

This is the point of the Blue Book passage. The picture of dead signs needing animation by an inner sense generates the search for something the practice already supplies. Once we see what mastery of a word actually consists in, the immaterial sense becomes idle. What gives the sign life is the role it plays in a language game embedded in a form of life — the trained, shared, ongoing activity of using it.

The use account is not merely a negative thesis. It rejects the essentialist picture by exposing a structural vulnerability that no unified definition is available for most ordinary terms, but it also offers a positive account of what understanding consists in. To grasp the meaning of a word is not to perform an isolated act of ostension that fixes a referent. It is to be inducted into a language-game. Understanding shows itself in the ability to participate: to apply the word in new circumstances, recognize its proper occasions, accept correction, and go on with others. The pain example illustrates this directly. A child in pain initially cries out of biological urge, expressing the sensation through natural behavior that requires no language. The child does not learn the word "pain" by attaching a label to a private inner object via inner ostension. Rather, adults teach the child to use "pain" as an extension and elaboration of this natural expressive behavior — "the verbal expression of pain replaces crying and does not describe it" (PI, 244). The child is drawn into a practice that includes natural reactions, contexts of injury and comfort, the question "where does it hurt," and the responses of others. Mastery of the word consists in fluent participation in this practice, not in any inner act of associating a sign with a sensation. This positive account does the work the essentialist picture cannot. It explains how meaning is shared (because the practice is public), how it is taught (through training, not private definition), how it can be gotten wrong (by failing to participate as the practice requires), and how it remains stable across novel applications (through ongoing participation in a form of life).
